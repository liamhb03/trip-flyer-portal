> Unlike most industrial sectors, commercial aviation has never had to rebuild its energy base. Since the birth of commercial flight, the same class of fuel, kerosene refined from crude oil, has powered essentially every aircraft in the sky. This is not simply a matter of habit or an inherited supply chain. Aircraft engines, airport fuelling infrastructure, and international certification regimes are all built around a narrow specification window, and any substitute fuel must slot into that window rather than force the industry to redesign around it. That constraint has kept aviation's energy base essentially unchanged even as other heavy industries, from utilities to shipping, have diversified toward multiple energy carriers over the past two decades.
>
> This structural rigidity is now colliding with a wave of binding European climate policy. Mandates such as the ReFuelEU Aviation Regulation and the phase-out of free allowances under the EU Emissions Trading System are forcing a shift toward sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) on a fixed timetable, regardless of whether the underlying supply chain, cost structure, or production capacity is ready to support it. The full detail of this regulatory architecture, and how it interacts with Dutch national policy, is set out in Section 3, immediately below. What matters for the argument here is more basic: the mandate does not, by itself, create a viable supply chain, and a supply chain is not the same thing as a business model. Bidmon and Knab (2018) describe the pre-existing arrangement among fuel producers, distributors, airports, and airlines as an industry recipe, a collectively stabilised logic of value creation and capture that has held for decades. Regulatory pressure is now placing that recipe under direct challenge, but it says nothing about what should replace it, who should build it, or how the costs and risks of the transition should be shared.
>
> This paper examines that gap through a specific circular innovation pathway: waste-to-SAF via hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL), a technology that converts organic waste streams into a biocrude intermediate that must then be upgraded into jet-range molecules. A pilot collaboration between the University of Amsterdam and Schiphol Airport has demonstrated that HTL can produce fuel-grade output from airport waste at lab scale. What it has not demonstrated, and cannot demonstrate through chemistry alone, is that this pathway can become a commercially viable operation. Technical feasibility and business viability are different questions, and the gap between them is not primarily a technology problem. It is an organisational and inter-organisational one: who invests, who bears the risk if a batch fails to meet specification, who certifies the output, and who is willing to pay a premium for it.
>
> The research question that follows from this is: how does a policy-driven innovation get translated into a viable, sustainable business model within the aviation ecosystem? This breaks down into five more specific questions. First, what does the current aviation fuel business model look like, and where do value and risk currently sit within it? Second, what structural elements of that model have to change to accommodate a waste-to-SAF pathway? Third, which actors have to participate in that change, including actors that play no role in the conventional fuel chain today? Fourth, how are the resulting costs and risks likely to be redistributed across the value chain? And fifth, can this transition happen without breaking the operational continuity the sector depends on, or does it necessarily introduce friction and disruption along the way?
>
> Answering these questions draws on three sources that have so far developed largely apart from one another: a review of the business model innovation and sustainable and circular business model literature, a mapping of the regulatory instruments governing SAF and waste valorisation at EU and Dutch level, and early interview material gathered from across the Dutch aviation fuel ecosystem. Section 2 sets out the methodology behind all three. Section 3 covers the regulatory architecture in detail. Section 4 reviews the business model innovation literature. Sections 5 through 8 build toward an integrated analytical framework drawing on the ecosystem, coalition formation, and financial viability literatures. Section 9 turns to the interview material. Section 10 discusses what the literature and the interviews together tell us. Section 11 concludes.

> ## 2. Methodology
>
> This review combines three strands of work: a systematic literature search on business model innovation and sustainable business model theory, a targeted regulatory mapping of the instruments governing waste-to-SAF pathways in the EU and the Netherlands, and a set of semi-structured interviews with stakeholders positioned at different points of the Dutch aviation fuel value chain. The literature and policy work ground the analytical framework developed in Sections 4 through 8. The interviews, reported in Section 9, provide early empirical evidence against which that framework is read in the discussion in Section 10.
>
> ### 2.1 Literature search strategy and databases
>
> Two independent databases were searched, Business Source Premier (EBSCO) and Scopus, covering the period from database inception through May 2026. Search terms were structured using a Venn diagram logic with two circles, an initial design that separated a theoretical circle covering business model and sustainable business model literature from a phenomenon circle covering aviation sustainability literature. The phenomenon circle was later removed from the combined query in consultation with a research team member, since it introduced excessive noise from aeronautical engineering literature, and aviation-specific terms were instead folded into a dedicated intersection query. The theoretical circle (Circle 1) combined a block of business model innovation and transformation terms with a block of sustainability and circular economy terms, searched across subject headings, titles, abstracts, and author keywords in EBSCO, and across title, abstract, and keyword fields in Scopus. The intersection query (Sustainable Business Models in Aviation) combined four blocks covering business model terminology, fuel and feedstock terminology, aviation terminology, and sustainability and decarbonisation terminology. Wildcard truncation and proximity operators were used to capture term variants, for example word-distance operators of three words to capture phrases such as emissions reduction or carbon minimisation. Filters were applied for English language, peer-reviewed status, and academic journal or article document type. Record counts were logged at each stage, with Business Source Premier Circle 1 returning 2,526 records, Business Source Premier Intersection returning 29 records, Scopus Circle 1 returning 2,274 records, and Scopus Intersection returning 341 records.
>
> ### 2.2 Deduplication and screening
>
> All records were exported to Zotero for reference management. Manual deduplication in Zotero proved too slow given the volume of records, so an automated deduplication tool (zoplicate) was used instead. The combined pre-deduplication set totalled approximately 5,170 records, reduced to 4,301 records after deduplication. The deduplicated set was uploaded to ASReview for screening, with title coverage at 100 percent, abstract coverage at 99 percent, and DOI or URL coverage at 92.6 percent. Records were screened and labelled as relevant, not relevant, or left unlabelled. The ABS Academic Journal Guide 2024 rankings were then applied, retaining only journals classified in categories 1 and 2, which reduced the set to 224 papers. A quality check was performed on the next 200 records in the ASReview ranking, continuing until 50 consecutive irrelevant records were reached, followed by team discussion of borderline inclusions.
>
> ### 2.3 Reference checking
>
> Backward and forward reference checking was conducted on key included articles, following citation chains to and from foundational sources such as Foss and Saebi (2017) and Pieroni et al. (2019). This step surfaced additional articles not captured by the original search strings, which were then screened using the same relevance criteria.
>
> ### 2.4 Regulatory and legal instrument mapping
>
> Because the case study sits at the intersection of business model theory and a dense regulatory environment, a separate mapping exercise was conducted for the legal and policy instruments governing SAF and waste valorisation. This mapping followed a trickle-down approach, starting from the ReFuelEU Aviation Regulation as the anchor instrument. Each operative article of ReFuelEU was read in full on EUR-Lex, and cross-references within the text were followed outward to related instruments. This process led from ReFuelEU to RED III for feedstock eligibility, to the EU ETS Directive and its Monitoring and Reporting Regulation for carbon accounting, to the Waste Framework Directive for waste classification, and to the EU Taxonomy Regulation for sustainable finance criteria. Implementing and delegated acts referenced within these primary instruments were retrieved and reviewed in the same way. The same trickle-down logic was applied at the Dutch level, starting from national instruments that transpose or sit alongside the EU rules, drawing on wetten.overheid.nl for statutory texts and open.overheid.nl and relevant ministry websites for policy documents. Where a Dutch instrument referenced an EU directive or regulation, or vice versa, that link was followed to confirm how the national instrument transposes or operationalises the EU-level rule. This citation-following approach was chosen over a keyword search of legal databases because the regulatory architecture is deeply cross-referential, and a keyword search risked missing implementing detail held in cross-referenced articles rather than in the primary instrument itself.
>
> ### 2.5 Interview approach
>
> Alongside the literature and regulatory mapping, five semi-structured interviews were conducted with stakeholders positioned at different points of the aviation fuel value chain, following the interview guide developed for the ENLENS project (reproduced in the appendix). Participants were selected to cover distinct functional positions in the value chain rather than for statistical representativeness: an infrastructure and logistics actor, an airline fuel buyer, a former employee of a SAF producer working specifically on the voluntary certificate market, a technology supplier to SAF producers, and a major integrated energy company active in both SAF production and trading. Table 1 summarises the participants. Interviews were conducted between April and June 2026, lasted between 20 and 55 minutes, and were recorded and transcribed with participant consent under the informed consent procedure described in the project's information sheet. All participants were offered pseudonymisation; where this document names a participant's employer, that reflects the participant's own choice on the consent form or a role so specific to one organisation (for example, board membership of a named joint venture) that anonymisation was not meaningful. Interviews are treated as illustrative rather than statistically representative, given the small sample size and the early stage of the broader ENLENS project, which anticipates further interviews with feedstock suppliers, certification bodies, and Dutch policymakers in later phases.

> ## 9. Interviews: Early Evidence from the Field
>
> ### 9.1 Overview
>
> This section reports on five interviews conducted for the ENLENS project, described in Section 2.5 and summarised in Table 1. The interviews were chosen to sample distinct functional positions rather than a single actor type, on the reasoning that a business model transformation of this kind cannot be read off any one actor's account. Read together, the five interviews surface a consistent pattern that recurs across every part of the value chain: a mismatch between the short-term, low-commitment way the conventional fuel market has always worked and the long-term, high-commitment way a pre-commercial SAF pathway needs to be financed. Each sub-section below reports one interview; Section 9.7 draws out the cross-cutting findings before the discussion in Section 10 reads this material against the literature.
>
> ### 9.2 Port infrastructure: enabler, not fuel actor
>
> The Port of Amsterdam interview offers the clearest illustration of how far regulatory pressure now travels beyond the actors it directly obligates. The interviewee was clear that the port authority sits outside the fuel transaction chain entirely: production, storage, and blending are handled by private terminal operators, with the port's role limited to the physical and logistical conditions, land, pipeline access, jetties, and berth capacity, under which those actors operate. Even so, the port's own investment planning is shaped by the same transition. The interviewee described an ongoing discussion with a fuel producer about an e-SAF production facility, an existing biodiesel operation run by private companies at the port, and a hub concept being developed with terminal operators for barge, rail, and pipeline import of bio- and e-fuels.
>
> Blending emerged as a specific chokepoint. Kerosene delivered to Schiphol arrives already blended to final specification, which means blending has to happen upstream at the terminals, not at the airport. This is complicated by the fact that bio- and e-kerosene do not arrive in the same steady, homogeneous volumes as conventional kerosene from a refinery: they come from multiple, comparatively small production plants whose batches can differ slightly in composition, so terminals have to keep incoming volumes segregated in smaller tanks until a batch can be blended to specification. The interviewee did not see this as an investment bottleneck in itself, since terminals holding a five-year offtake contract will typically invest in the additional tank capacity needed, but the absence of such contracts at the moment means investment decisions are effectively on hold.
>
> Three constraints were repeatedly identified as more binding than terminal capacity itself: production-side access to green electricity and where production should be sited given how sharply renewable electricity availability varies by geography; the length of the path to financial closure for new production plants, described as taking five to six years from initial concept to output, linked to producers' difficulty securing offtake commitments from airlines unwilling to pay a premium above the regulatory minimum; and the certification and paper trail associated with SAF, described as complicated under current EU and national rules, a burden reported to the port secondhand through KLM rather than experienced directly. Asked directly which actors are essential to make SAF viable, the interviewee named airlines, airports, production plants, and the public sector, but was consistent in declining to position the port authority as a coordinating or lobbying actor in its own right, repeatedly redirecting questions about certification, blending standards, and regulatory detail to KLM, Schiphol, or the terminal operators. It is a limitation of this interview that the informant's own regulatory knowledge was general rather than detailed, which is itself informative about where policy literacy sits across the ecosystem.
>
> ### 9.3 KLM: SAF as cost, not business model
>
> The airline interview is the most direct evidence in the interview set that a mandate does not automatically produce a business model. The interviewee, responsible for M&A and holdings at KLM and a board member of a SAF joint venture the airline co-founded, was explicit that KLM sees no real value creation from SAF adoption beyond the ordinary appreciation of its minority equity stakes in producer companies. In his words, it is "just a cost for us... just cost increasing," with no business model behind the shift itself.
>
> Two changes since the ReFuelEU mandate came into force were described as material. First, until 2025 KLM could sell the CO2 credits generated by SAF use into the road transport sector under the old HBE unit system, which offset a meaningful share of the cost; that route closed once aviation itself became mandated, so KLM now bears the full price differential. Second, voluntary sales of SAF to passengers and corporates remain very low even though stated willingness to pay in surveys is much higher, a gap the interviewee attributed partly to a legal constraint: court rulings mean KLM can no longer market SAF as "sustainable," which the interviewee linked directly to reduced ability to sell a green premium to end customers.
>
> The most detailed material concerns project finance. A typical SAF project moves through three phases: a pre-feed feasibility phase costing roughly three to ten million euros, a feed phase to reach construction-ready status costing thirty to one hundred million euros, and construction itself costing five hundred million to five billion euros. Financing the feed phase, the middle phase, is described as the binding constraint, because project debt (roughly 70 percent of financing) is only available once a fixed, long-term offtake contract removes revenue risk from the project. This forces airlines into ten to fifteen year offtake commitments before a plant is built, a wholly different undertaking from the one-year fuel contracts airlines are used to. KLM's response has been to help set up a joint SAF investment fund with other airlines, its fuel supplier, and financial partners, explicitly to outsource technical due diligence on producer projects that KLM lacks the internal capability to assess project by project; the interviewee estimated fewer than five people internally are genuinely knowledgeable about SAF finance. On policy, the interviewee argued for treating SAF siting as a European rather than national question given feedstock and renewable electricity availability, and suggested redirecting EU ETS carbon-price revenue toward closing the SAF price gap rather than letting it flow into general government revenue. Asked how costs are likely to be distributed, the interviewee put the split at roughly 50 to 90 percent passed to the passenger depending on how much of a level playing field exists internationally, with the remainder absorbed by the airline.
>
> ### 9.4 The voluntary and certificate market: a second, disconnected market
>
> The interview with a former Neste employee who worked specifically on the company's emission-reduction solutions business surfaces a distinction largely absent from the rest of the draft: a physical SAF market and a separate voluntary certificate market that operates alongside it under different rules. In this model, the physical fuel is sold to airlines at Schiphol and burned in the ordinary course of operations; separately, the emission-reduction value of that fuel is decoupled and sold as a certificate to corporate buyers, typically firms with high business-travel footprints and limited ability to buy physical fuel directly, such as consultancies and banks. Certificates are issued only after the underlying fuel has actually been burned by a named airline partner, and explicitly cannot be counted by the airline itself toward its own ReFuelEU mandate; the two markets are kept legally and administratively separate.
>
> The interviewee described this voluntary market as immature relative to the underlying physical market. Terminology is unsettled (SAF certificate, carbon credit, and several company-specific names are used interchangeably), and corporate sustainability teams were, at the time, waiting for the Science Based Targets initiative's aviation scope 3 guidance to be finalised before committing budget, since it was unclear whether a given purchase would count cleanly toward a company's own emissions targets. The interviewee also pointed to a lack of public benchmarking: several corporate clients had made purchases but declined to publicise them, out of concern about greenwashing accusations, which in turn slowed the emergence of the peer pressure and case studies that might have accelerated broader uptake. Cargo and freight buyers were noted as an emerging, more willing segment relative to passenger-side corporate travel.
>
> ### 9.5 Technology supply: following the customer through the value chain
>
> The interview with a catalyst manufacturer supplying SAF producers, including a long-standing collaboration with Neste on HEFA catalyst, illustrates how a transition of this kind propagates upstream to actors with no direct fuel market exposure. The interviewee described the change inside the company less as a new division and more as a reallocation of an existing research and development portfolio: increased attendance at SAF-specific conferences, renewed participation in EU consortium projects after a lapse, and two current EU-funded projects, one on lignocellulosic feedstock to SAF via pyrolysis and one on algae to SAF via hydrothermal liquefaction, the same conversion technology used in the ENLENS HTL pilot, though applied there to plastics rather than algae.
>
> The interviewee's account of the company's role changing across technology readiness levels is a useful complement to the dynamic capabilities material already in the literature review: at low readiness levels the company acts in an advisory capacity, since it does not fund fundamental research itself, and its commercial role grows as a pathway approaches manufacturing scale, since catalyst supply at scale is where the company actually earns revenue. On feedstock, the interviewee flagged a cross-sectoral dynamic not otherwise present in the draft: the ethanol industry, facing declining gasoline-blending demand as road transport electrifies, is actively repositioning toward alcohol-to-jet SAF as an alternative outlet for the same production base, and a similar reconsideration is underway in parts of the paper and pulp industry regarding biomass streams. On investment horizon, the interviewee estimated ten to twenty years as the period the company needs before committing to a joint technology development effort, tied explicitly to how much clarity exists on the regulatory framework a given pathway will operate under. Geography of partnership was described as driven more by technology fit than by national policy specifically, since the company's customers operate internationally regardless of where a given plant is sited.
>
> ### 9.6 An integrated energy major: risk allocation as the core problem
>
> The Shell interview is the most theoretically direct of the five, and largely in its own terms restates the central argument this paper is making from a practitioner's seat. The interviewee, responsible for partnerships and technology in Shell's low carbon fuels business, framed the entire transition as a problem of allocating a set of risks, market risk, technology risk, asset risk, feedstock supply risk, CO2 policy risk, project financeability risk, and insurance risk, among actors who each, in his account, tend to see only their own side of the exposure. He offered his own metaphor for the practical consequence: bringing the value chain's actors into a viable business model, in his words, requires something closer to a marriage counsellor than a commercial negotiator, because the disagreement is less about numbers than about each party failing to see the other's risk position as legitimate.
>
> The specific mechanism he described lines up closely with the horizon mismatch KLM's interviewee also raised, from the opposite side of the negotiating table: producers need ten to twenty year offtake commitments to secure project debt, while airlines are structured, financially and organisationally, around six-month to one-year fuel purchasing. He pointed to the EU's proposed double-sided auction mechanism and the UK's Revenue Certainty Mechanism, both already referenced in this paper's policy section, as direct government responses to exactly this mismatch: a public or quasi-public intermediary signs the long-dated contract with the producer and offers airlines a shorter, more familiar commitment, absorbing the horizon risk itself. He also described extreme risk-spreading behaviour among the interviewee's own banking counterparties, citing a Dutch SAF project financed by a consortium of eleven banks as an example of lenders themselves refusing to hold concentrated exposure to a single pre-commercial pathway.
>
> On Shell's own strategy, the interviewee described a deliberate pattern of entering a new fuel or feedstock market first as a trader, to build customer relationships and market knowledge, before committing to production investment, which he offered as the reason Shell stepped back from a planned HEFA production plant while continuing to expand its SAF trading book. He was candid that value capture shifts along the chain over time depending on the relative scarcity of feedstock versus processing capacity, a dynamic that echoes the catalyst supplier's account of HEFA feedstock and processing capacity converging. On new entrants, he distinguished established peers such as BP, Total, and Eni from a larger population of unproven project developers whose commercial pitches were not always matched by delivery capability, and gave a vivid account of the cultural gap between Shell's petrochemical safety culture and the far more informal operating norms of some agricultural-sector feedstock partners, a point that maps closely onto the boundary-work and culture-gap material already covered in Sections 5 and 6 of the literature review. Asked for his own view on policy design, he argued that the EU's feedstock- and pathway-specific rules (Annex IX sub-categories, differentiated treatment of HEFA versus e-SAF versus advanced pathways) are, in his words, more prescriptive than the outcome-based carbon-intensity approach used in California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard, and that this granularity raises cost and slows volume without a clear offsetting benefit, citing an industry estimate that e-SAF currently runs ten to fifteen times the cost of fossil jet fuel. He flagged this as a personal view rather than a company position, and noted a political affordability risk if the cost of the transition rises faster than public willingness to pay for it.
>
> ### 9.7 Cross-cutting findings from the interviews
>
> Three patterns recur across all five interviews and are picked up again in the discussion below. First, every interviewee who was asked, independently, identified the mismatch between short-term fuel purchasing norms and the long-term offtake commitments a pre-commercial project needs as the central practical obstacle, described in KLM's terms as a project finance problem and in Shell's terms as a risk allocation problem, but pointing at the same mechanism from opposite ends of the negotiating table. Second, actors with no direct fuel transaction exposure, the Port of Amsterdam and the catalyst supplier, both described their own investment and R&D planning as shaped by the same mandate and the same offtake uncertainty that constrain producers and airlines, while both also declined to see themselves as responsible for resolving that uncertainty. Third, the interviews surfaced a second, largely separate voluntary certificate market operating in parallel to the mandated compliance market, governed by different actors (corporate sustainability buyers, standard-setters such as the Science Based Targets initiative) and different rules, which does not appear anywhere in the literature reviewed in Sections 4 through 8 and represents a genuine gap between the academic framing of SAF adoption and how the market actually operates on the ground.

> ## 10. Discussion
>
> ### 10.1 Reading the interviews against the co-evolution literature
>
> Wasserbaur et al. (2022) describe the relationship between government policy and business models as one of co-evolution rather than simple cause and effect: regulation shapes value propositions, cost structures, and revenue models, but firms and ecosystems also adapt to, exploit, and reshape the instruments available to them. Three of the five interviews illustrate this from different points in the chain. The Port of Amsterdam is not a regulated party under ReFuelEU in the way fuel suppliers and airlines are, yet its own investment planning is nonetheless being shaped by the same regulatory signal, mediated through its commercial relationships with terminal operators and producers who are directly obligated. KLM's interviewee described the withdrawal of the pre-2025 HBE credit-selling route as a direct, first-order example of an instrument change reshaping the airline's cost structure overnight, exactly the mechanism Wasserbaur et al. describe. Shell's interviewee went further, describing Shell's own trading-before-producing strategy as a deliberate exploitation of regulatory and market signals rather than a passive response to them, which is closer to the active, reshaping side of co-evolution than either of the other two accounts. Read together, the three interviews suggest co-evolution is not evenly distributed across the ecosystem: better-resourced, more integrated actors like Shell can actively reshape the instruments they operate under, while infrastructure and demand-side actors like the port and the airline are more often reacting to a fixed signal.
>
> ### 10.2 Barrier, facilitator, or niche innovator
>
> Bidmon and Knab's (2018) three-role typology, in which business models in a sustainability transition act as barriers, facilitators, or independent niche innovations, helps locate where the interviewed actors currently sit. The Port of Amsterdam's account places it closest to the facilitator role: not resisting the transition, not itself the site of innovation, but its infrastructure decisions are what allow niche production such as the HTL pathway under study, or the e-SAF facility under discussion, to scale if demand materialises. KLM's account is harder to place cleanly. The interviewee's own framing, that SAF adoption is "just cost" with no change to KLM's underlying business model, reads as closer to the barrier role than KLM's public sustainability commitments would suggest, and is a useful corrective to any assumption that a mandated actor is automatically acting as a facilitator. Shell's dual role as both a large-scale trader and an active technology and project investor places it across two of Bidmon and Knab's categories simultaneously, facilitator through its trading infrastructure and financing activity, and a would-be niche innovator through its own production and licensing investments, which suggests the typology may need to be applied at the level of a specific activity within a firm rather than to the firm as a whole for a diversified actor like Shell.
>
> ### 10.3 The bankability and offtake mismatch, seen from three sides
>
> The interviews strongly corroborate two established findings from Sections 6 and 7 of the literature review, and add detail that the literature does not fully capture. Overholm's (2017) claim that bankability is the binding constraint before other alliance-formation problems can be addressed is echoed in near-identical terms by both KLM and Shell, from opposite sides of the same negotiation: KLM describes the ten to fifteen year offtake commitment project finance requires as a fundamentally different undertaking from ordinary fuel purchasing, and Shell describes the same mismatch as the central risk allocation problem the whole sector needs to solve. The chicken-and-egg coordination problem described by Hellstrom et al. (2015) and the European Commission's Sustainable Transport Investment Plan is visible here in more operational detail than the literature provides: KLM's account of the pre-feed, feed, and construction phases, and its identification of the feed phase specifically as the point where projects stall for lack of financeable offtake, gives the abstract coordination problem a concrete shape. What the interviews add beyond the literature is that the party with the physical infrastructure, the Port of Amsterdam, does not see coordinating this standoff as part of its remit even though its own storage and blending decisions depend on the standoff's resolution, and that government-backed intermediary mechanisms (the EU's proposed double-sided auction and the UK's Revenue Certainty Mechanism, both raised independently by Shell's interviewee) are now the concrete institutional response to exactly this gap, a development that postdates most of the literature reviewed in Section 7.
>
> ### 10.4 A second market the literature does not cover
>
> None of the demand-side literature reviewed in Section 7.4, including Gössling and Humpe (2023), Karanki and Yu (2026), and Muller and Wittmer (2026), addresses the voluntary certificate market described in the interview with the former Neste employee, in which SAF's emission-reduction value is decoupled from the physical fuel and sold separately to corporate buyers under standards such as the Science Based Targets initiative's aviation guidance. This is a genuine gap rather than a minor omission, since it means a second demand channel, governed by different actors and different rules than the ReFuelEU mandate, is currently operating alongside the mandated compliance market with no clear treatment in the academic literature on SAF adoption economics. Given that Muller and Wittmer (2026) already identify corporate willingness to pay as constrained by internal budget authority rather than preference, the existence of a parallel voluntary certificate market that struggled, at least at the time of interview, with unsettled terminology and unclear standards suggests the corporate demand-side story is more fragmented, and less well captured by a single willingness-to-pay figure, than the current literature implies.
>
> ### 10.5 Modular change at the edges, architectural change at the core, revisited
>
> Applying Foss and Saebi's (2017) modular versus architectural distinction to the fuller interview set sharpens the pattern identified in the earlier draft using only the port interview. The port and the catalyst supplier both describe the changes required of them, additional storage and blending capacity in the port's case, a reallocated research and development portfolio in the catalyst supplier's case, as extensions of existing capabilities rather than a redesign of how they create or capture value. This reads as modular change in Foss and Saebi's terms. KLM's and Shell's accounts point to architectural change concentrated further upstream and in the financing structure itself: KLM's description of a joint investment fund built specifically to outsource project due diligence, and Shell's description of a fundamental reallocation of risk across the chain, both describe firms restructuring core relationships and capabilities rather than extending existing ones. This uneven distribution of change intensity, light at the infrastructure and upstream-supply layer, heavy at the financing and offtake layer, bears directly on the paper's fifth research question: operational continuity looks genuinely achievable at the port, terminal, and technology-supply level without major disruption, but the interviews suggest continuity is far less certain for producers and airlines working through the offtake and financing bottleneck, which is where Axelson et al. (2021) would predict the deepest business model change to concentrate.
>
> ### 10.6 Limitations of the discussion at this stage
>
> This discussion remains provisional. Five interviews across five distinct roles is a broader base than the single interview the earlier draft relied on, but it is still a small, purposively selected sample, and several participants' own regulatory knowledge was general rather than detailed in areas outside their direct remit. The reading offered here should be treated as a set of working hypotheses to be tested against further interviews with feedstock suppliers, certification bodies, and Dutch policymakers, which the ENLENS project anticipates conducting in a later phase, rather than as a settled account of how co-evolution, coalition formation, and architectural change are playing out across the Dutch SAF ecosystem as a whole.

## F. Conclusion

**Insert directly after Section 10 (E above), still before `References`.**

> ## 11. Conclusion
>
> This paper set out to examine how a policy-driven circular innovation, waste-to-SAF via hydrothermal liquefaction, can be translated into a viable, sustainable business model within the aviation ecosystem. The literature reviewed in Sections 4 through 8 establishes that this translation is not a technical problem but a coordination problem operating simultaneously at the firm, inter-firm, ecosystem, and institutional level, and that circular business model transformation of this kind requires architectural rather than modular change precisely because no single actor controls the feedstock, the conversion technology, the certification infrastructure, and the offtake market at once. The interview evidence presented in Section 9, and discussed in Section 10, is consistent with this picture and adds concrete texture to it. Across five interviews spanning an infrastructure actor, an airline, a former SAF producer employee, a technology supplier, and an integrated energy major, the same underlying mismatch recurs: the long-term commitment a pre-commercial SAF project needs to become bankable does not fit the short-term way fuel has always been bought and sold, and no single actor in the chain, including actors with strong public sustainability commitments, currently sees resolving that mismatch as their own responsibility. This combination, a mismatch felt by nearly every actor paired with no actor clearly accountable for closing it, is arguably the central obstacle this paper has identified, echoing and sharpening the finding of the earlier draft that relied on a single interview.
>
> The contribution of this work is to bring together a literature on sustainable and circular business model innovation that has developed mainly around manufacturing and energy-utility contexts, a literature on SAF adoption economics that treats business model largely as a sector-level construct, a detailed regulatory mapping of the EU and Dutch instruments that govern this specific pathway, and a first empirical pass across five distinct points in the Dutch aviation fuel value chain, and to apply the resulting framework to a case, HTL from airport waste, that does not yet appear in either literature. In doing so it also surfaces two points the literature has not yet addressed: a voluntary certificate market operating alongside the mandated compliance market with its own actors and standards, and a methodological point, that measuring readiness for this kind of transition requires asking not only whether the technology works and whether the policy mandate is binding, but whether any actor in the ecosystem sees itself as responsible for assembling the coalition the mandate presupposes will simply appear.
>
> This remains a limitation as much as a finding. The empirical base at this stage is early, one case study and five interviews, and the analytical framework in Section 8.2 has not yet been tested against interviews with feedstock suppliers, certification bodies, or Dutch policymakers. The next phase of this research will extend interviews across these actor types, track how the coalition around the UvA-Schiphol HTL pilot develops as it approaches commercial scale, and follow closely the resolution of specific open questions identified in this review, including the outcome of the EU's book-and-claim feasibility study, the CMP's treatment of HTL as a processing route for sewage sludge, the design of the EU's proposed double-sided auction mechanism and its UK counterpart, and whether any Dutch e-SAF or advanced bio-SAF project reaches final investment decision in the period ahead. Aviation's decarbonisation depends on regulatory mandates converting into real production capacity, and that conversion, this paper argues, will be decided less by chemistry than by who is willing to build, and pay for, the coalition the mandate assumes.
